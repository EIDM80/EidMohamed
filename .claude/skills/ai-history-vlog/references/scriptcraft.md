# Scriptcraft — how to write a crazy-good history-vlog script

**This is the single highest-leverage part of the whole pipeline.** Locations, face-lock, 1080p — all
table stakes. What makes an episode go viral vs. get scrolled past is the *writing*. Spend the most
effort here, and don't start generating until the script genuinely rips. When in doubt, rewrite.

The job: make a viewer feel like they're *there*, learn something that reframes how they see the past,
and feel something by the end — in a voice that sounds like a real overwhelmed human, never a narrator.

---

## The 6 laws (non-negotiable)

**1. One burning spine question.** Every great episode is secretly answering ONE question the viewer
wants answered ("How does a nine-year-old become a living god — and then get erased?"). Seed it early,
starve it through the middle, pay it off at the end. If you can't say the spine in one sentence, the
script isn't ready.

**2. Hook = drop into a reaction, mid-overwhelm.** The first 3 seconds decide retention. Never an intro
("Welcome to…", "Today I'm in…"). Start already reacting to something specific and slightly wrong/
mysterious. The first line should imply *stakes or a mystery*, not context.
- ✅ "—sorry, okay, give me a second. …that temple. That's *gold*. That's actually painted gold."
- ❌ "I've travelled back to Thebes in 1332 BC, the capital of the New Kingdom."

**3. Teach through friction, never lecture.** The best facts arrive when the host *bumps into a problem*
and the reaction IS the lesson. He tries to pay → discovers money doesn't exist → we just learned the
barter economy, and it was fun. A fact stated is forgettable; a fact *discovered* is a moment.
- ✅ "So I went to pay him and I've got— there's no money here. It hasn't been *invented*."
- ❌ "Egypt used a barter economy measured in the deben weight standard."

**4. Plant 3–5 'wait, WHAT?' beats.** These are the shareable moments — genuinely surprising, verifiable
truths that *reframe an assumption*. Myth-corrections are gold (the ruins were painted & gilded; the
god-king was a sick kid; he was erased then became the most famous human face ever). Rank them; the
biggest becomes the closing line, the rest get sprinkled + go on the fact-cards.

**5. Ride an emotional wave — never one note.** Map the feelings across the runtime:
disorientation → wonder → delight/comedy → human connection → awe → quiet melancholy. A 3-min piece
that's all "wow" is boring by 90s. Contrast is what makes each beat hit.

**6. Close on dramatic irony — the line people quote.** The host knows the future; the people on screen
don't. End on the gut-punch that reframes everything you just watched. Quiet, over the landscape.
- "Nobody here knows this kid dies at eighteen. That his own people erase him… and three thousand years
  from now, he's the one we remember. Right now he's just a boy in a big gold chair."

---

## The human voice (ban the AI tells)

The dialogue must sound like a real person talking to their phone while stunned — **messy, specific,
reacting in real time.**
- **Fragments & self-interruption.** "Wait— why is everyone— …oh." Real speech breaks mid-thought.
- **React to what's happening NOW**, including to NPCs mid-sentence.
- **Concrete & sensory > abstract.** "Look at her *hands*" beats "she is grinding grain."
- **Understate the huge stuff.** Let the fact do the work; don't oversell it.
- **BANNED (instant AI smell):** "Here's the crazy part," "here's what gets me," "basically," "little
  did they know," "the richest/most powerful X on Earth" as an opener, and tidy symmetrical sentences
  ("It's not X, it's Y"). If a line sounds like a documentary VO or a textbook, cut it.
- **Rhythm:** vary sentence length wildly. One awe beat, one real laugh, one warm human moment per
  episode, minimum.

## Local interactions (warmth + life + comedy)

Give each act one small exchange with a period local — a vendor, a baker, a scribe — who **acts and
says a short beat**, and the host reacts naturally. This is what makes the past feel *alive* instead of
a museum diorama, and it's where the humor and heart live (the baker hands him hot bread; a vendor
calls him "Nefer"; the scribe hands him the brush and he draws a shaky bird). Keep NPC lines to a beat.

## Curiosity gaps / open loops

End beats on a small hook that pulls to the next one, so there's never a natural place to stop watching.
"…and that's when I noticed everyone around me had gone completely silent." Cut to the next scene.

---

## Scene-writing formula (repeat per beat, ~28s each)

For each of the ~7 scenes write:
1. **WHERE** — the location/plate.
2. **THE DISCOVERY** — the one surprising thing he bumps into here (the beat's reason to exist).
3. **THE FRICTION/INTERACTION** — the problem or the local exchange that *delivers* the discovery.
4. **THE LINE(S)** — natural, first-person, in the voice above (this is what gets spoken/generated).
5. **THE LESSON (implicit)** — what the viewer walks away knowing, delivered *through* 2–4, never stated.
6. **THE HAND-OFF** — the open loop or emotional shift into the next scene.

## Finding the story in the research

Before writing, mine the era for these five and build the script from them:
- **Myth-corrections** — what's surprisingly different from the popular image (→ the "wait, WHAT?" beats).
- **The one human at the center** — a meetable figure whose *specific, human, surprising* detail lands
  (Tut: nine, frail, ~130 canes, married his sister, restored the gods, erased, then world-famous).
- **The dramatic irony** — what the people don't know that we do (→ the close).
- **The 3–5 shareable facts** — verifiable, reframing, cross-checked against 2 sources.
- **The sensory texture** — sounds, smells, food, money, clothing (→ the friction & local beats).

## Micro-example (before → after)

> ❌ AI-flat: "Thebes was the wealthiest city of its era, and interestingly, its temples were brightly
> painted rather than the bare stone we see today."
>
> ✅ Alive: "…okay, wait. That's not stone. The whole thing's *painted* — blue, red, actual gold leaf.
> Every temple you've ever seen as a grey ruin? To these people it's a city drowning in colour. Nobody
> tells you that."

## The bar

Read the finished script aloud. If any line sounds like a narrator, cut it. If there's no moment that
makes *you* go "wait, WHAT?", the history isn't surprising enough — dig deeper. If you don't feel
something on the last line, the close isn't landing. **Do not generate until it passes all three, and
get the user's sign-off on the script first.**
