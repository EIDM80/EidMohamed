Topic: How Does Wi-Fi Actually Work?

Core idea: Wi-Fi feels invisible and magical, but it's just radio waves carrying
digital information — the same physics as an FM radio station, tuned to a
different frequency and carrying a different kind of signal.

Key points to cover:

1. Your router is a tiny radio transmitter and receiver. It broadcasts on
   specific frequency bands — mainly 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz (and increasingly
   6 GHz with newer standards).

2. Data doesn't travel as "WiFi" in some special form — it travels as
   electromagnetic radio waves. Your device's wireless chip encodes digital
   bits (1s and 0s) by rapidly varying properties of that wave — its
   amplitude, phase, and frequency. This encoding scheme is what lets a huge
   number of bits ride on a signal that, physically, is just an invisible
   wave rippling through the air.

3. The two main bands trade off range vs. speed:
   - 2.4 GHz: longer wavelength, travels farther, penetrates walls better,
     but is slower and more crowded (shared with Bluetooth, microwaves,
     baby monitors).
   - 5 GHz: shorter wavelength, faster, less crowded, but drops off faster
     over distance and struggles more with walls.

4. Why walls and distance hurt your signal: radio waves lose energy
   (attenuate) as they spread out and as they pass through or reflect off
   materials. Dense materials (concrete, metal, water — including the water
   in a human body) absorb or reflect more of the signal than wood or
   drywall. This is why moving one room over, or even just standing in the
   wrong spot, can tank your connection.

5. Interference: Wi-Fi shares its radio spectrum with other devices.
   Overlapping signals from neighboring routers, or other 2.4 GHz devices,
   collide and corrupt data, forcing retransmission — which is what shows up
   to you as lag or buffering, not just "weak signal."

6. The handshake: when your phone "connects" to Wi-Fi, it's doing a
   back-and-forth negotiation with the router — authenticating, agreeing on
   encryption keys, and establishing a channel — all before any of your
   actual data (a webpage, a video, a message) starts flowing.

7. The bigger picture: once your data is on the router, Wi-Fi's job is
   basically done — the router hands it off to a wired connection (ethernet,
   fiber, cable) that carries it the rest of the way to the internet. Wi-Fi
   is only the last short hop, wirelessly, between your device and that
   router.

Takeaway: Wi-Fi isn't magic — it's a radio conversation happening constantly
between your device and your router, encoded cleverly enough to carry video,
voice, and data, but still bound by the same physical limits as any radio
signal: distance, obstacles, and interference.
